The Greenwood's Heart — Final Outline
Total target: ~10,000 words across 10 chapters
Register: romantasy-explicit, female-gaze throughout, mainstream-published heat level
Project: MOIST pipeline novelette, Pacific Northwest historical romantasy, autumn 1897
Format: beat-level outline for prose-pass generation

Project Notes
This is the spiced compilation of The Greenwood's Heart — the female-gaze pass on the original Cedar Sparrow skeleton, rebuilt across ten chapters with the sensual register dialed up from the opening line. Every chapter is built around her body, his body, and the slow simultaneous awakening of both. The supernatural structure (matched marks, the fae bond made visible) is the structural keystone; the romance is the magic, the rite is the romance, the body of the world ratifies the body of the bed.
The outline is built for prose-pass rendering. Each beat is specific enough that a generation pass can stage the scene; intentionally short of full prose so the model has room to play.
Voice register: Long-breath sentences, frank vocabulary where it serves, occasional formal cadence (especially Thistle's), small comic moments allowed inside heavy ones. Era is late nineteenth-century Pacific Northwest; speech and interior should feel period-flavored but not affected.
Sensuality register: Mainstream-romantasy-explicit. Bodies are bodies. Acts are described frankly in outline form for the prose pass to render. Female gaze on every character of romantic, narrative, or aesthetic significance, principal cast and side cast alike.

Cast
Thistle-in-the-Hollow
Two hundred years old in fae years. In her human glamour she is small and slender — five-foot-two, the long-limbed-and-lean slim that fae bodies run to, with the cool fae-warmth of skin that has never been weathered. Ash-blonde hair to her hips, slightly waved, almost too fine to be human. Eyes the green of sun through new leaves — when she is undone, gold runs in faint threads through the iris. Freckles in bark-patterns down her sternum, her ribs, the insides of her thighs, the underside of her arms, the tops of her feet. The patterns shift when she is affected, then settle into a fixed shape after Ch. 9. Mouth full at the lower lip, often slightly parted; she has not learned to hide her face. Long throat. Sharp collarbones. Small high breasts the shape his hand cups perfectly when he learns where to put his hand. Narrow waist. Hipbones visible at the curve where the bark-pattern freckles run. Long legs — lean rather than muscled, the long line of a creature who walks twenty miles a day on moss. Small bare feet with the same bark-pattern freckles across the tops; her toes slender, her arches high. She does not own shoes. She does not understand why anyone would. Her wings, glamoured away most of the book, are iridescent in a way that does not stay one color when light moves on them — closest comparison is a damselfly's underwing in sun.
Caleb Daniel Thorne — "Cal"
Thirty years old in 1897. Six-foot-two, the working build of a logger who has been swinging an axe since he was twelve — broad through the shoulders, the kind of waist a working man keeps without trying, forearms corded and vein-mapped from the haft of an axe. Dark hair, slightly too long, falls forward when he bends. Dark beard kept just short of unkempt. Eyes green-grey — closer to green in good light, closer to grey in foul. Hands large enough to span Thistle's wrist with the thumb covering her pulse. Calluses at the heel of each palm. Dark hair across his chest, dense at the sternum, tapering down. A long pale scar at his ribs from where Samuel saved him from a falling cedar when Cal was sixteen. A second smaller scar along his eyebrow from a chain that broke when he was twenty-two. After the rite (Ch. 8): a thin gold seam under the skin from the heel of his left thumb up the inside of his wrist along the vein Thistle traced with one finger on Day 1 of Six Days — visible always, fading to silver in certain slants of light by Ch. 10. A bruise-shape on his sternum, the print of her splayed hand, present from the night of the woods onward — faded in Ch. 10 to a pale gold-tinted shadow visible only in low light.
Samuel Daniel Thorne
Cal's younger brother, twenty-three at the time of the events. The schoolteacher version of Cal — same shape of face, same set of the mouth, but softer, slighter, scholarly. Five-foot-eleven. Lean rather than broad. Same dark hair, kept shorter and combed back; falls into his eyes when he is reading and he pushes it back without noticing. Eyes brown rather than green-grey. Wire-rim glasses he has been wearing since he was fifteen, settled into the bridge of his nose with a small permanent dent. Long-fingered hands — teacher's hands, Cal calls them, with no irony — clean and unmarked, with a faint ink stain at the side of his right middle finger that he never quite scrubs off. The kind of handsome that does not announce itself — the kind women in the back of his classroom in Portland notice in the third week of term and then cannot stop noticing.
Aunt Morrow
A crow-fae of indeterminate age, considerably older than Thistle. In half-form: tall, severe, dark-haired with the early grey that fae sometimes have when they have grieved a long time. Sharp features, sharp black eyes carried over from her crow shape. Beautiful the way an old falcon is beautiful — not soft. Wears the feathered shoulders of her crow form even in half-form, a black cape of living feathers that lifts in the wind. She lost her Thomas a hundred years ago to a logger and a musket; the loss is in her face every time she looks at Cal until Ch. 10.
Hiram Beck
Miller Company foreman. Forty-eight, the kind of man who has used his body for thirty years and never for any kindness. Six-foot-three. Broad as a barn door across the shoulders, the working bulk of a man who has felled three thousand trees and never met a tree he respected. Sandy-blond hair going grey at the temples, kept short. Pale blue eyes — good eyes, which makes the cruelty in the mouth more frightening. Stubble that he does not bother to shave at the start of a six-week contract. A broken nose from a fight in 1882 that healed wrong. Big hands, scarred across the knuckles, the size of a man who has knocked other men down for sport. The kind of attractive that women in town cross the street to avoid — physical authority without the consent of the people standing near him. He is the version of Cal that the world tried to make Cal into. Cal punches him once at the end of Ch. 7 and Hiram does not get up.
Mae Pritchard
Twenty-six. Postmistress at Hollow Creek. Red hair pinned up in the back-office style of women working in offices in 1897, a few strands always escaping at the temple. Freckled across the bridge of her nose and the high points of her cheeks. Blue eyes. Slender, soft at the hip, the comfortable kind of pretty that has not been told it is pretty often enough. Married Eli Brennan the blacksmith in February 1898. By the epilogue she has a daughter and Cal has eaten two of her custard pies without irony.
Eli Brennan
The Hollow Creek blacksmith. Twenty-nine. Six-foot, blond going to brown, blue-eyed, the easy-tempered kind of handsome that does not know it is handsome. Broad-shouldered from his work; soft-handed in the way a man can be when he has loved his work since he was apprenticed at fourteen. Beard like wheat. Mae calls him honey in front of customers and he does not blush; he just smiles at the floor.
Albert Gould
Miller Company clerk. Sixty-one. White-haired, slim, dignified — the kind of older man who is unembarrassed by his own kindness. Salt-and-pepper moustache. Strong forearms still, from a young manhood in the forge before he switched to bookwork. Has known Cal since Cal was twelve. Knows what is happening in his ledger and chooses, on the relevant day, not to act on it.
Daniel Thorne
Cal and Samuel's father. Deceased winter 1875. In Cal's memory: tall, dark-haired, dark-bearded — the original of the body Cal has grown into. Hands like Cal's hands. Quiet the way Cal is quiet. Loved a woman who died of grief the spring after he did.
Marin Whitlow Thorne
Cal and Samuel's mother. Deceased spring 1876. In Cal's memory: small-framed, dark-haired, freckled across the nose. Gave Samuel a tin whistle the year before she died. The name Cal does not say aloud for twenty-two years until he says it three times into a tree's wound at the rite in Ch. 8.
Bract
Cal's one-eared dog. Mixed breed, mostly border collie. Six in 1897, eight in 1898 with grey on his muzzle. Lost the left ear to a barn cat at one. Devoted to Thistle from the moment she puts her bare hand on his head in Ch. 2.

Chapter 1 — What She Has Been Doing (~800 words)
Setting: Late afternoon at an active logging cut near Miller's Camp, then dusk at the heart-tree clearing.
POV: Thistle throughout.
Beats:

Cold open. Thistle in the underbrush twenty feet from Cal, glamoured invisible, on her belly in the ferns with the cool earth against her small bark-freckled feet and the cold of it traveling up the inside of her thighs through the thin summer dress. He is alone in the cut — the rest of the crew gone to the creek for water. He is shirtless. His suspenders hang at his hips. There is sawdust caught in the hair on his chest and a sheen of sweat at the base of his throat that catches the light every time he lifts the axe. She has been here for an hour and a half. She has work she is supposed to be doing. She is not doing it.
Establish what she has become. She has done this for nineteen days. She knows where he goes to mark trees, where he eats his noon meal, where he stops to drink. She has watched him take his shirt off in this clearing four times. The first time, a small grove of fiddleheads unfurled out of season in the moss beneath her. The second time she made a sound she had never heard her own mouth make. The third time she went home to the hollow at the Mother's roots and put her own slender hand under her own dress in the dark and was so badly surprised by what happened that she did not move for an hour after. She is two hundred years old. She has tended this forest since before his great-grandfather was born. She did not know her body could do this. She thinks about it constantly. She is here, in the ferns, with the front of her dress pressed flat to the cool moss for reasons she will not say aloud even to the trees.
The thing that makes him not just a body. He stops. Sets the axe down. Walks to a young cedar — straight, healthy, four feet across, the kind of tree a logger marks first. He stands in front of it for a long moment. Then he lifts both hands and presses his palms flat to the bark, surrendering. He drops his forehead to the trunk between his hands. She hears him, very quietly: "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I don't know how to stop." He stays like that for the count of ten. Then he straightens, picks up his chalk, and marks the tree for felling anyway. Something in her sternum unmakes itself and makes itself back wrong. He is not just a beautiful animal. He is breaking. She has been wanting a man who is also breaking and she did not know that was what she had been wanting until now.
The near miss. He walks toward the stream — toward her. She does not move. He drops to one knee not six feet from where she lies, cups water in his hands, and washes his face, his throat, his chest. A bead of water tracks from the hollow of his throat down to his sternum and disappears into the dark hair at his ribs. Her glamour ripples; she feels it go. In the moss between his knees a single white trillium pushes up out of the dirt and opens, full and obscene and out of season, in three breaths. He sees it. He stops with one hand half to his face. He says, very softly, "…what." He picks the flower. He turns it in his fingers. He looks around. He looks exactly at the patch of fern where her face is, and her two-hundred-year-old fae heart stops doing what it is supposed to do for the count of three. Then he shakes his head, tucks the trillium in the breast pocket of his discarded coat, and goes back to his axe. She does not start breathing again until he has been gone five minutes.
The theft. When he leaves at the day's end, his leather glove is on the stump where he had been sitting. She takes it. She has taken three things from him in nineteen days — a length of bootlace, a horn button torn off in a snag, now this glove. The leather is still warm from his hand. She does not put it in her satchel. She puts it inside the front of her dress, against her sternum, against the bark-pattern freckles, where the heat of him can transfer directly to her skin. She walks back to the Mother that way.
The Mother knows. Dusk at the heart-tree clearing. Thistle presses her palm to the bark to report — but her cadence is gone, her voice is wrong, and the Mother answers with a pulse of warmth that is almost amused. Like a grandmother who has counted nineteen nights and is waiting to be told. Thistle sinks to her knees at the roots and presses her face into the hollow where she keeps her human things. The bootlace. The horn button. A brass button from a year ago that has no story. She holds the new glove to her mouth and breathes him in until her wings flicker against the back of her dress and a soft sound escapes her that she has never made in this clearing in two centuries.
Aunt Morrow. Lands as a crow on a low branch. Shifts to half-form mid-drop — tall, severe, the black feathered cape across her shoulders settling. Sees the glove in Thistle's hand before Thistle can hide it. Sees the freckles at Thistle's throat reordering themselves into something that is not a forest pattern. A long silence. Then, quiet and broken: "Oh, girl. Oh, no." Then, harder, the warning: "The Miller men have marked the eastern stakes. Axes by the new moon. Do not go near them. I lost my Thomas to a musket and a smile, and he was only a man. This one is —" She stops. Both of them know what this one is. Morrow's black crow-eyes go wet. "Put the glove back where you found it, Thistle-in-the-Hollow. Tonight. Before you cannot."
Thistle's steel. She tucks the glove deeper into her dress instead. "If they come for the Mother, they will not leave." Morrow makes a sound like a sob and a laugh and a curse all at once.
The cry. From the east — far off, in failing light — a man's voice shouts once in pain, and is cut off. She knows the voice. Of course she knows the voice. She has known it for nineteen days. Morrow caws — "Don't you DARE, girl, don't you —" — and Thistle is already moving, the glove still pressed to her sternum, her bare feet hitting the moss in a rhythm the forest has not felt from her in a hundred years.
Closing image. Her, running east. The Mother behind her, watching. Morrow above her in crow form, keeping pace, scolding the whole way in a language only Thistle understands. The trillium that Cal tucked in his coat pocket, two miles ahead, still open in the dark.


Chapter 2 — Sorry, Sorry (~1,000 words)
Setting: Eastern edge of the Greenwood, dusk bleeding to full dark.
POV: Thistle, with one short paragraph in Cal's delirium.
Beats:

The finding. She comes through the trees at full run, his glove still pressed flat to her sternum inside the front of her dress. She finds him pinned under a widow-maker the size of a horse — a rotten cedar limb across his thigh — his fingers clawed into the moss, his shirt torn open from collar to navel where he tore strips for a tourniquet. The body she has been watching for nineteen days. The body she has fallen asleep imagining against her own. Broken. There is blood crusted at his thigh and sap-pitch in the hair on his chest and a fly walking on his open mouth. Something in her that has lived two hundred years discovers, in the half-second of seeing him, that it has been a coward.
He is still talking to the tree. "Sorry. Sorry. Didn't mean to crowd you. Sorry." The same word she heard him press into the cedar four hours ago in the clearing. He has been here, alone, apologizing to the limb that crushed him, for hours. Her fae heart does something that is almost rage and almost weeping. Fae law says let the forest take him. He marks trees for the Miller Company. He is everything she has been warned about for nineteen days. Her hands are already on the branch before she has finished the sentence in her head.
The lift. Full fae strength. Her glamour drops in a sheet — wings half-unfurled, eyes the wrong green, freckles in patterns that match no human pattern. She does not even brace her bare feet. She throws the limb six yards into the brush. Underestimate-her payoff #1. He does not see it. He is too far gone to see anything.
The healing — central sensual set piece of the chapter. She has to put her hands on him. She has to choose where. The break is in his thigh, high, mid-femur. She pushes the torn trouser-fabric aside with a hand that is no longer two-hundred-year-old steady. Her slim palm goes flat against the bare skin of his upper thigh — the dark hair there, the heat of him, the size of him under her hand — and she has the small, surprised understanding that she has tended deer and bear and once a cougar but has never in her life touched a man's bare leg, and that her body has been making this distinction in the dark of her hollow every night for nineteen nights without telling her what it was making. Green light at her fingertips. The sound of growing things. The bone knits — she feels it knit under her palm, the strange intimacy of a body remaking itself against her hand, and she is breathing through her teeth.
Her other hand. She does not have to put her other hand on his bare chest. She does it anyway. She tells herself it is to steady him while the magic works. It is not. It is so she can feel his heart under her palm. It is hammering. It hammers harder when her hand lands. He moans, low, half-conscious, the sound of a man and not a wound, and her wings shudder out further behind her in answer before she can call them back.
The scar at his ribs. Her thumb finds it without her permission — a long pale line under the dark hair of his chest, an old one, well-healed. She has watched him from twenty feet for nineteen days and never seen it. She presses her thumb along its length, slow, once, like a woman reading. He shivers under her hand. She makes herself stop. She does not, in fact, stop right away.
He opens his eyes. Green-grey. Unfocused. She is leaning over him with one hand high on his thigh and one hand spread flat on his chest and her wings half-out behind her in the dusk. He sees her. He breathes one word: "Angel." It lands in a place inside her that she did not know had a name. She answers, formal and shaking, in the voice she uses for the Mother: "I am as real as you are kind."
One paragraph in his head. I am dying. This is what dying is. She is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen — small and bark-freckled and lit from inside — and her hand is on my chest and if I die now it is all right. I'm sorry, Sam. I tried. Her eyes are the wrong color of green and I am not afraid. He closes his eyes.
Bract. Bract arrives at a dead run from the tree line — one-eared, frantic, mud to the chest. He sees her crouched over Cal and does not growl. He stops two feet away and sits. She holds out the hand that has just been on his master's chest. Bract pads to her and presses his head into her palm. She laughs — wind-chime, low, surprised, her first laugh in the story — and Cal, eyes closed, says, just audibly: "Do that again." She does. She laughs once more, just for him, soft enough that the trees lean in to hear. Bract leans his entire weight against her thigh and does not move from there for the rest of the scene.
The carry. She gets her arm under his shoulder. He gets his arm around her waist — his fingers fisting hard into the fabric at her hip, his face turning into her hair without permission, his breath at her throat. He should not be possible for her to lift; she is small and he is enormous. She lifts him anyway. He smells of sawdust and blood and sweat and the cedar smoke that has been on the glove pressed against her sternum for nineteen days, and she has the realization, walking in the half-dark with his weight against her side, that those nineteen nights of breathing the leather had been preparing her for this. The actual man. The actual heat. It is not the same. It is so much worse, and she would not unmake one of those nights.
The trailhead. She gets him to a stump where two lantern-bearing loggers will find him in twenty minutes. She lowers him against the wood. He does not let go of her dress at her hip for a long moment. He says, hoarse: "Don't —" and does not finish the sentence. She will not know, for nine more days, what was supposed to come after the word.
The glove falls. As she kneels to settle him, the leather glove that has been against her sternum for the run east comes loose and tumbles down the front of her dress into his lap. His own glove. The one he has been missing for six days and not been able to remember where he set down. He looks at the glove in his lap. He looks at her. The dusk is thick and his head is full of static; he cannot be sure of what he is seeing. She reaches for it. He moves first.
The wrist-hold. His hand closes around her wrist before her fingers reach the glove. His hand is enormous around it. His thumb lands on the inside of her wrist, on her pulse, and stays there — by accident, but it stays. He does not let go. She cannot make herself take her wrist back. She has been alive for two hundred years and she has never been held by anyone. His thumb on her pulse is doing something to the rest of her body that she has no vocabulary for. The freckles down her sternum, under the open laces of her dress, reorder themselves silently into a shape she will not see until she gets home and looks in the rain basin: the print of his hand, splayed, where her hand has just been on his chest.
Names. "Name?" She cannot lie. "Thistle." "I'm Cal." "I know." Two words. She has known his name for nineteen days. She has held his glove to her mouth in the dark of her hollow. She does not say so. She does not have to. He sees something move in her face, and his thumb presses harder against her pulse for one full second before he lets her go.
She leaves the glove with him. She means to take it back. She gets to her feet. She walks four steps. She turns. He is watching her. He is holding the glove in the hand that just held her wrist. She makes herself not return for it. She is giving him the thing she has been falling asleep against for nineteen nights and she does not know yet what she has done.
The lanterns. She melts into the tree line just as two men come up the trail shouting his name. She watches them find him. She watches them lift him onto a makeshift litter. She watches him turn his head once, slow, looking for her, before they carry him out of sight.
Closing image. Thistle in the tree line at full dark. Her wrist still cinched in the heat-shape of his hand. She presses her own slim fingers there, hard, like she is trying to hold the print in. Bract somewhere behind her, having stayed with Cal where a good dog stays. Her sternum bare of the glove for the first time in nineteen nights. Above her, somewhere in the dark, a single crow has been watching the entire scene without speaking, and the crow's silence is louder than any warning Morrow has ever given her.


Chapter 3 — The Kettle and the Straight Razor (~1,000 words)
Setting: Six days after the woods. Cal's one-room cabin a half-mile from Miller's Cut. Stove, bed, table, one chair, Bract's rag rug, a shelf of whittled birds, the leather glove on the peg by the door where he has been looking at it for six days.
POV: Cal.
Beats:

What six days have done to him. Open on Cal at dusk, splitting wood he does not need split. He has been chopping for two hours. His shirtless back streaked with sweat. His shoulders shake. He keeps chopping. He has done this every evening since the woods. He works himself to a place where his body cannot dream, and his body dreams anyway. He wakes at three in the morning with his right hand closed in a specific shape around nothing — the shape of a wrist he held once — and does not know what to do with the hand for the rest of the night. The bone in his thigh, which by the camp doctor's accounting should be six more weeks healing, is healed. He has been pressing his thumb along the line where the break was and finding nothing but smooth skin. He has stopped looking in the small shaving mirror because there is a mark on his chest where her hand was, faint, the color of a bruise, and he is not ready to know what it is.
The glove. It has been on the peg by the door for six days, leather, fitting no one. He has not moved it. He has touched it three times. The third time he held it to his face like a man tasting weather. He put it back. He has not told himself why.
The knock. He knows who it is before he opens the door. He shouldn't. He does anyway. He stops chopping at the third stroke of the knock and stands in his own kitchen with the axe still in his hand and understands, in his body, what he is about to do. He sets the axe down. He crosses the room. He opens the door.
What she is wearing. She is on his porch in a dress that is wrong — too thin for autumn, summer fabric, the cut of it from a generation ago, fitting a slim frame that the dress was not measured for. Bare small feet on his porch boards. Hair loose to her hips. Her hands are empty. She has not come with the glove. She has not come with anything. She has come with no reason and she is not pretending to have one. The collar of the dress is high — old-fashioned high, throat-covering, fastened with three small buttons up under her chin. She is hiding something. He looks at the collar. He looks at her face. He does not ask. He says, "Come in," and does not recognize his own voice.
The threshold. She steps past him into the cabin and her shoulder brushes his chest. Her hair, in the half-second of her passing, against his throat. His whole body locks. Bract comes around the door, sees her, and sits at her feet without a sound, his tail thumping once. Cal closes the door behind her. There is nowhere in the room she can stand that is more than five paces from him.
She does not pretend. She looks at everything — the stove, the shelf of whittled birds, the rag rug, the bed in the corner. She lets her eyes rest on the bed for a moment. He sees her let them rest. She lets her eyes come back to him. She does not look down. She has been looking at him for nineteen days and now they are six feet apart with a closed door and she is not looking down. He has never been looked at like this in his life. He has the discovery, standing in his own kitchen, that being looked at is a thing that can be done to a man.
The chair, the blanket, the stew. He gives her the chair. He gives her his only blanket — wool, his, smelling of him — and she wraps it around her slim shoulders and presses her face to it for half a second over the collarbone, the wool against her mouth, and her eyelids drop. He sees her do that. He has to turn to the stove and stay turned for longer than the stew needs. He cooks. He nearly burns the onions because he is being watched while he cooks for the first time in his thirty years and his hands are not behaving. She is delighted by his hands not behaving. She does not hide that she is delighted.
The kettle. She lifts the lid like it is an oracle. He explains tea. She is rapt. He has never been listened to like this. He has been listened to to verify and to correct and to take orders from. He has not been listened to to know him. He has to keep talking because she keeps looking at him and the only thing he can think of to do with his mouth, in front of her, is words.
The razor. She has crossed to the wash-shelf while he ladles stew. She picks up his straight razor and opens the blade flat against her small pale palm. He sees her in profile — the blade on her skin, the line of her bare throat above the high collar, the small fae figure of her in the lamplight — and he crosses the cabin in two strides. He does not take the razor from her. He closes his hand around hers, his fingers over her fingers, both of their hands closed on the steel together. His face six inches from hers. He says, "That'll take your finger off." She says, looking at his mouth, "I would grow another." He does not pull the razor away. They stand like that — hands together on a blade, breath together in the half-foot of air between them — for three seconds, then four. She is the one who lets go. He puts the razor back on the shelf and finds that his own hand is shaking.
The bowl. He gives her his bowl because there is only one. She eats two bites. She holds the bowl across the table to him. "Together." He does not know how to refuse. They share. Her spoon. His spoon. Her spoon again. He has not eaten in three years without thinking of the cost of the food. He eats now without thinking of anything but her mouth on the spoon.
The forearm. He reaches across the small table to take her bowl back when she has finished. His sleeve rides up — his forearm bare to the elbow, the corded muscle, the vein at the inside of his wrist. She looks at it. She does not look up to check whether he sees her looking. He sees her looking. He has never been looked at this part. He cannot speak. He sets the bowl down. He does not pull his arm back. She lifts one slender finger — one finger — and lays it along the line of the vein at the inside of his wrist, light, like a woman reading. His pulse hits her fingertip and jumps. She holds her finger there for the count of three. She withdraws her hand. She says, very quietly, "I am sorry. You are very —" She does not finish the word. He does not know which word it was. He will think about it that night.
The question. She asks, formal and devastating, looking at his hands on the table: "Do you love your work, Caleb Thorne?" He chokes on his tea. No one in his life has asked him that. He says, "No." He says it looking at her bare wrists where the blanket has fallen open, and it is the most honest thing he has said in three years.
The wandering. He washes the bowl. She gets up. She walks the small circle of his cabin — touches the shelf of whittled birds, touches the table, touches, finally, the edge of the bed. He sees her touch the bed. He does not breathe for a moment. She does not look at him while she does it. She has done it on purpose. She wants him to see her do it.
The hat. She comes to the leather glove on the peg first. She does not touch the glove. She does not need to touch it. They both know where it has been. Above the glove is his hat. She lifts the hat down. She puts it on her own head. She turns to him. Her ash-blonde hair under the brim. The high collar. The summer dress in autumn. Her small bare feet on his floor. She does not take the hat off. He cannot speak for a moment. He has the small clear thought, I am going to die in this cabin. He says, very quietly, "Keep it." She does not. She lifts the hat off her head, slow, and puts it back on the peg. But the moment is on the table now. They both saw it.
The bed. He gives her the bed. He takes the floor. The fire is low. The room is small. She lies on her side facing him on the floor and watches him not sleep. He lies on his back and counts the boards on the ceiling. Twice he hears her breathing change. He does not look. Around two in the morning she makes a small sound in her sleep — half a word — and he sits up. She is asleep. She has one small hand pressed flat against her own sternum under the blanket, over the high collar, over whatever it is the collar is hiding. He sits up the rest of the night. He does not lie back down.
Morning. He is in the chair by the stove with two cups of tea ready when she opens her eyes. She looks at him in the morning light. He says, "Good morning." She says, "Caleb." She does not say anything else. They drink tea in silence and the silence is louder than anything either of them could have said.
The tree line. He walks her out. She turns to go and then turns back. She crosses the three steps between them. She is on her feet before Morrow is awake. Morrow caws — "Girl, wait, GIRL —" Thistle is already running. Not to the cabin. To the clearing. The Mother is at the clearing. He is at the clearing. Both of the things she loves are at the clearing and the crew is coming and she has slept through the night she had to use.
Closing image. Cal's cabin door swinging shut behind him at first light. His axe over his shoulder. Bract at his heel. Three objects in his coat: the iridescent scale, the cedar sparrow, the dried trillium. On the porch step behind him: three trillium flowers, brown and closed. One alder leaf, fallen. He starts up the trail toward the eastern ridge with no plan in his head except find her, find her, find her, and the forest opens a path for him without asking why.


Chapter 7 — The Axe and the Foreman (~1,200 words)
Setting: The heart-tree clearing. First light through cold mist. A dozen lanterns. The Mother. The crew. The axe.
POV: Cal.
Beats:

Cal alone with her. He arrives at the clearing forty minutes before the crew, still walking — running has made his ribs hurt and slowing has not helped. The mist is at his chest. The Mother is in front of him for the first time in his life. He has marked her on a map. He has discussed her felling under canvas at a foreman's table. He has not seen her. Nine feet across. Two hundred and fifty feet of red cedar going up into mist that hides her crown. A thousand years standing in this exact eight feet of dirt. He understands, in his body, what he was going to do. He understands it the way a man understands a cliff he has been driving toward in fog. He puts his palm flat on the bark, the way he has watched Thistle do, twice, through a window he was not supposed to be looking through. The bark is warm. The Mother knows him. Of course she knows him — she has been getting his apologies through her daughters and her granddaughters and her great-great-grandchildren-trees for three weeks. He kneels. He does the only thing he knows. He says, into the bark, "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know. I'm sorry." He stays there with his forehead against the trunk and his three objects in his coat — the scale, the sparrow, the dried trillium — until the lanterns come up the trail.
The crew. Twelve men. Lanterns. The two-man saw on a sledge. Hiram Beck in front — six-foot-three, broad as a barn door, sandy-blond going to grey at the temples, pale blue eyes, the kind of physical authority that does not ask for consent, his face the red of a man either drunk last night or drunk now. The men go quiet when they see Cal kneeling at the base of the tree he has been hired to fell.
Hiram's voice. Loud, performative — for the men. "Thorne. Get up. We've got six weeks of work and you're praying to a tree." Cal does not get up. The men shift their feet. Hiram crosses the moss. "Pick up your axe, Thorne. I won't ask twice." Cal stands. He puts his back against the Mother. He says, quiet, the voice he uses to spook horses: "Hiram. The contract is broken. Take the men back to camp. I'll go down to the office today and surrender it."
The cruelty. Hiram's face works. He has been waiting for this. He has been waiting for Cal for three years — the foreman's quiet pet who out-cuts him every quarter and who reads in the bunkhouse and who apologizes to trees. Hiram smiles, and the smile makes his good blue eyes uglier. "You've gone soft, boy, because you've been tupping a witch." The crew goes still. Hiram raises his voice. "Don't pretend, Thorne. The men saw the flowers around your cabin yesterday. Out of season. Five kinds, in November. We've all been waiting to see how long it'd take you to admit it." He turns to the crew and spreads his arms like a preacher. "There's a witch in his cabin, boys. Has been since he came off the widow-maker with a busted leg miraculously healed." He turns back to Cal. He lets his voice drop. "Miller Company don't pay for witchcraft. Pick up the axe, Thorne, or I'll send a runner to the sheriff in Hollow Creek for her. And one to Portland for your brother's school. Take your choice. The witch or the boy."
Cal's silence. This is the moment. Cal does not speak. He looks at Hiram. He does not blink. He has been a quiet man for thirty years and the silence in him goes all the way down. The men have never seen him like this. Two of them — younger ones, boys really — step backward.
Thistle arrives. She steps out of the tree line on the eastern side of the clearing. Full fae. Glamour gone. Wings half-unfurled — iridescent in the mist, six inches taller than her human shape, eyes the green of sun through new leaves, freckles in bark-patterns down her sternum and her bare arms and her throat where the high collar of her dress has come open at the laces. Hair loose to her hips. Small bare feet on the moss. She has run from the spring at fae speed and her body is still failing to make a clean human shape. She is terrible in the cathedral way — the way that makes men understand they are small. Two men in the back drop their tools and run into the mist. They will be telling this story for the rest of their lives and no one in Hollow Creek will believe them.
What Cal sees. The print on her sternum that was the bruise-shape of his hand at dawn this morning is gone. Just freckles in bark-patterns. The matched mark is no longer on her body. He sees it. He understands it. He has not had time to understand it — his head fills with white static — and then she is across the clearing, and there is no time.
The standoff, three voices. Cal: "Thistle. Go home. Go home, Thistle, go home." He is trying to save her from what is about to happen. He uses the voice he uses to boys and to soldiers. He does not use the voice he uses to her. Thistle, formal: "Caleb Thorne. The Mother is my home." Hiram, smiling: "There she is. Look at her, boys. Wings and all. Now tell me again, Thorne, that I'm not earning my keep." He spits into the moss. He says, soft, "Last time, Thorne. Pick. Up. The axe."
Cal does not pick up the axe. He turns his body fully toward Thistle, his back to Hiram, his back to the Mother. He has decided. He says, looking only at her, "Thistle. Behind me. Get behind me." She does not move. She has been alive for two hundred years. She does not get behind humans.
Hiram picks up the axe. Cal's axe. The one Cal taught Thistle to hold on Day 3, hands over hands, his chest against her back, his thumb on her wrist. That axe. Hiram lifts it off the moss with the practiced ease of a man who has felled three thousand trees in his life. Cal sees him do it. Cal moves toward him — three strides — but Hiram has already turned to the Mother and is already swinging and the swing is the swing of a thirty-year veteran and there is no time, no time —
The strike. The axe bites the Mother. Deep — a full foot of bite, into the heartwood, the wound the width of a man's torso. The sound is not a sound. It is what a green thing makes when it understands it is being killed: a low rending creak in the air and a higher pure note under it that does not travel through the ear but lands in the chest, behind the sternum, in the place a man keeps the names of the dead. Every man in the clearing feels it. Bract howls and does not stop howling.
Thistle falls. Not over — down. Her wings dim mid-air and fold in on themselves like something put away. She lands in the moss face-first and does not move. The freckle-patterns on her bare arms fade and re-form wrong, disordered, broken birch-bark into nothing. Gold sap runs from the corner of her mouth onto the moss. The same gold the Mother is running from her wound. The same gold. The body of the tree and the body of the woman are bleeding the same thing.
Cal's silence breaks. He has not spoken in two minutes. The sound that leaves him is not his voice. It is the voice of a man who has not allowed himself to be angry in three years and who is angry now. He crosses the moss faster than he has ever crossed any moss in his life. Hiram is still raising the axe for the second stroke. He does not get the second stroke. One punch — a logger's punch, three years of swung axes, his entire weight through his shoulder — square in Hiram's temple. Hiram drops. The axe falls beside him. He does not move. He is not dead. He will be in a cell in Hollow Creek before noon and he will not stop talking about witches, and no one in the territory will believe him, because his face will look exactly like what it is: the face of a man who got hit by Caleb Thorne.
The crew scatters. Nine men in nine directions through the mist. Some of them will tell the story badly. Some of them will keep their mouths shut. None of them will come back to this clearing for the rest of their lives.
Cal goes to her. He crosses the moss on his knees the last three yards. He gathers her up. She weighs nothing — she has always weighed nothing — but the less she weighs now is something he can feel in his arms, the absence of a breath under skin, the wrongness of a body whose animating thing has gone somewhere else. He presses his forehead to her temple. He says her full name three times into her hair, the only way he knows how to call her back: "Thistle-in-the-Hollow. Thistle-in-the-Hollow. Thistle-in-the-Hollow." The same name she said into his mouth at dawn. He says it the way he heard her say his — formal, full, an invocation. Her eyes do not open. Her lips do not move. The gold at the corner of her mouth has spread to her chin. He puts his thumb to it. It is warm. It is sap. It is her blood. He stops being able to think.
What he sees on her bare chest. The print of his hand on her sternum that he traced with his thumb at dawn — gone. Just freckles in bark-patterns. Just a body. He understands, in his arms, that the mark going into her means the bond is gone or going and he does not know the difference and he does not know how to bring it back.
The cedar sparrow. It has fallen out of his coat in the punch. It is in the moss six feet from his knees. He cannot stop for it. He cannot leave her for the three seconds it would cost. He looks at it. He leaves it.
Morrow. She lands on his shoulder in crow form. She does not shift. She does not have time. She caws once in his ear — go. He goes.
The run. Cal carrying Thistle. Bract at his heels. Morrow above. The forest opens the path the way it opened a path for him this morning, the second mercy in twelve hours, except that this one feels like a hand pushing him forward more than a kindness. He whispers apologies into her hair the whole way — not the careful sorry he has given to trees, the broken sorry of a man who finally understands what he is.
The spring. He lays her at the edge on the moss. Her hair across the moss the way it lay across his pillow at dawn. Her eyes closed. The gold sap at her mouth still. He puts his hand on her sternum and finds the print not there and makes a sound that the spring will remember for two hundred years. Then he does the thing he has been not doing since the woods. He pulls open the front of his own shirt. He looks. The bruise-mark on his own chest, the print of her hand that has been there since the night she healed him — gone. Just skin. The bond is dying in both bodies and it is dying together. Bract lies down at her feet and presses his head to her ankle and does not move. Morrow drops out of the air, shifts to half-form at last, lands on her knees beside her niece. She is openly weeping, which Thistle has never seen her do, which Cal has only seen one other woman do in his life (Mae Pritchard at her father's funeral, when he was sixteen). Morrow does not speak. She cannot speak yet.
Closing image and the line that ends the chapter. Cal on his knees. His hand on Thistle's sternum where his print used to be. His own shirt open over a chest where her print used to be. Morrow at her other side. Bract at her feet. The Mother somewhere behind them in the clearing with her wound open and her gold running into the moss. Cal looks up at Morrow, his face wet — he has not noticed his own face — and he says, "Tell me what to do." Morrow does not answer. He says it again. "Tell me what to do." He says it a third time. "Tell me what to do." Morrow opens her mouth.


Chapter 8 — A Named Sacrifice (~1,200 words)
Setting: The hidden spring, then the heart-tree clearing. Late morning into mid-afternoon. Sun finally through cedars.
POV: Cal.
Beats:

Morrow opens her mouth. "The Mother's heartwood takes a named sacrifice. A human, freely. Your true name, three times, over her roots. And a thing that costs you. All of it. Or she dies and my girl dies with her." Cal: "How long do I have?" Morrow: "You are already too late, logger. Try anyway." She is weeping while she says it. Her black crow-eyes are wet and she does not wipe them. He starts to say Caleb Thorne and she cuts him off: "Your true name, logger. Not the name the world calls you. The name your mother gave you." He understands. He says, slow, the way a man retrieves something from the bottom of a chest he has not opened in twenty-two years: "Caleb Daniel Thorne. Son of Daniel Thorne and Marin Whitlow Thorne. Born in Hollow Creek, the third of March, eighteen-sixty-seven." He has not said his middle name aloud since his mother died. He had not known he was still carrying it. Morrow nods once. "That will do. Bring her. Bring all of her. Do not leave her here." She lifts into the air and is a crow again before her feet leave the moss.
Carrying her back. He gets his arms under her. She weighs less than she weighed when he ran her here an hour ago. He carries her at a walk, not a run; running is what put the gold at her mouth and he will not put more there. Bract paces him. The forest opens the path the way the forest has been opening paths for him all day. Cal does not let himself think about that.
The clearing. The crew is gone. Hiram is gone — some of the men, or the sheriff's runners, or both, have hauled his body off the moss while Cal was at the spring. The cedar sparrow that fell from Cal's coat is sitting on the flat stone at the base of the Mother. Morrow brought it back, in her beak, while he was carrying. The Mother stands with her wound open. Gold sap running. The clearing is empty of every living small thing — no birds, no insects, no small movement in the leaves. The forest has cleared the room.
He lays her down. He arranges Thistle at the base of the Mother on the moss directly under the wound. He smooths her ash-blonde hair the way she wore it in his bed at dawn. He closes the front of her dress at the laces because he cannot bear for her bare chest to be looked at by anyone other than him, even by the trees. The bark-pattern freckles where his hand-print used to be go in under the linen and out of sight. He kisses her forehead. He kisses her mouth — through the gold sap at her chin. His own mouth comes away gold. He does not wipe it.
What he sets on the stone. He empties his coat. The iridescent scale she shed on his chair on her first night in the cabin. The dried trillium he pocketed in the cutting clearing twenty days ago without knowing why. The cedar sparrow he whittled while she slept beside him in his thoughts. He sets each thing on the flat stone. Then from his wallet: the last sixty dollars in Miller Company scrip — the money for Samuel's coach down to Portland. And from his breast pocket: the folded contract with GREENWOOD EASTERN RIDGE HEART CEDAR PRIORITY FELL stamped in red across the top. These are the things that cost him. He sets them next to the others.
The rite. Beat one: the contract. He strikes flint to tinder. He holds the small flame to the corner of the folded paper. The red stamp curls and blackens. He kneels with the paper between his hands until only ash. He says, looking at the Mother, "Caleb Daniel Thorne. Son of Daniel Thorne and Marin Whitlow Thorne." His voice is quieter than the wind that is not moving in the leaves.
Beat two: the scrip. He digs with his hands at the base of the wound where the gold sap is pooling. The earth is cold and wet. He presses the bills into the hole. He covers them. He pats the moss down over them. He says again, "Caleb Daniel Thorne. Son of Daniel Thorne and Marin Whitlow Thorne." His voice breaks on his mother's name. He has given his brother's rescue back to the forest he has been cutting for three years. He has no reason to believe this will work. He does it anyway.
Beat three: the small things. He gathers the trillium, the scale, the cedar sparrow, and lays them in a row on the moss directly under the Mother's wound. "This was hers before I knew her," he says of the scale. "This was mine before I knew her," he says of the trillium. He picks up the cedar sparrow. "This was both of us. I am giving it back." He sets the sparrow last.
Beat four: the cut. He draws his knife and opens his left palm in one clean stroke from the heel of his thumb to the heel of his hand. The blood is bright red, normal, the blood of a thirty-year-old logger who has bled into this forest a hundred times for nothing. He presses his palm flat to the axe-wound in the Mother's trunk, into the gold sap, into the deep heartwood gash. He says, the third time, "Caleb Daniel Thorne. Son of Daniel Thorne and Marin Whitlow Thorne. I'm hers. I'm yours. Take it. Take whatever. Take all of it. Just —" He does not finish. He does not know how to finish. He stays with his palm pressed to her wound.
What he sees on his hand. Where his blood meets the gold sap, his blood is turning gold. Not mixing — becoming. The change travels up the heel of his hand, into his wrist, under the skin, in a thin gold line along the vein she traced with one finger in the cabin. He watches the gold go in. He does not pull his palm away. He thinks, take it. He thinks, take all of it. Take me. Just give her back.
The forest answers. A sound like a great breath drawn in across the whole clearing. Wind moves without moving any leaves. The mist that has hung at chest-height since first light lifts in a single steady rise, like a curtain being raised by hand. The gold at the axe-wound runs faster, then brighter, then thickens. The bite in the heartwood begins to close — not heal, close, the wood knitting like flesh, the bark rings folding back over each other in front of his face. A long pale scar forms where a foot of axe-bite was. And from the closed scar, immediately, the way things happen only inside a story: a sapling pushes out of the bark, no taller than Cal's hand, and unfurls one new leaf. The leaf is the green of sun through new leaves. The green of her eyes.
He collapses. His knees give. He goes down with his face still against the Mother's bark. The bark is warm. For one moment he is held — the smell of cedar and gold sap and new growth, the body of the Mother breathing under his face the way Thistle's body had been breathing under his face six hours ago — and he weeps in a way that has been waiting since he was eight years old. He is not quiet about it. As his cheek presses to the trunk, he feels the matched bruise-mark return to his own chest under his shirt — a small warmth blooming in the shape of her hand against his sternum. He does not look down to check. He does not have to. Morrow does not look at him; she is looking at her niece.
What Morrow sees and does not say. Under the laces at the throat of Thistle's dress, the bark-pattern freckles on her sternum are reordering — slow, like ink rising into paper from the underside. Morrow watches the small splayed shape redraw itself on her niece's skin: the four fingers, the palm, the heel. The print is back. The matched mark is back. The bond has held. Morrow does not tell Cal. She lets him find it on Thistle's body in private. It is the only thing in the day that is going to belong only to them and Morrow will not take it from them.
They wait. Cal pushes himself off the Mother. He goes to Thistle. He kneels beside her. He puts the cedar sparrow back in her closed hand and folds her fingers around it. He puts his cut palm — the slow gold spreading in patches under the skin of his wrist — over her sternum, over the print he cannot yet see has come back. He bends and puts his forehead to hers and he waits. An hour. Two. The light shifts to early afternoon. The sapling at the Mother's scar puts out a second leaf, then a third. Bract has not moved from Thistle's feet. Morrow sits in half-form on a low branch and weeps silently.
Her fingers twitch. He feels it before he sees it. Her hand closing on the cedar sparrow. He does not move. He does not breathe.
Her eyes open. The green of sun through new leaves. She is looking at him — at him, not through him. Her mouth works. She lifts her free hand, weak, slow, and lays her fingertips against his lips. She says, "Caleb." The familiar name. The one that means I see you. I know you. I am still here. He breaks. He drops his forehead to hers. He says her full name back, three times, the way he said it into her hair in the clearing two hours ago and did not know was the rite — "Thistle-in-the-Hollow. Thistle-in-the-Hollow. Thistle-in-the-Hollow." — and she lets him say it.
The matched mark. She moves the hand that is not holding the cedar sparrow. She lays it flat over her own sternum, the place she has been laying her own hand for chapters and chapters. She knows it has come back. She does not have to look. She says, "You did it." Cal does not understand. He looks down. He sees her hand on her own chest, and he sees, under her fingers, the print of his own hand returned to her skin — the bruise-shape, the four fingers, the palm, the heel, the matched mark made visible again. He sits back on his heels. He says, "Oh." That is all he says. "Oh."
Morrow. She drops out of the tree. She does not come close. She stops three feet from them. Her voice is ragged from weeping. "Thistle-in-the-Hollow. You will not do that again." Thistle gives a small exhausted laugh — not the wind-chime, a smaller one, the laugh of a woman who has just been carried back from the place humans do not come back from — and says, "No, Aunt. I will not." Morrow looks at Cal for one long second. She does not speak to him. She turns and walks into the trees in her woman's body, not her crow's. Cal understands. She has just blessed him by not killing him. It is the most she can do today.
The Mother. Cal helps Thistle sit up against the trunk of her own Mother, the trunk that has just closed itself around his blood. The sapling at the new-pale scar has three leaves now and is leaning toward them. Thistle puts her palm flat on the bark beside the sapling and says, in her formal cadence, the voice she has not used to the Mother since the night she first heard a man cry out in the dusk: "Mother. I am sorry. I am sorry." The Mother answers the way the Mother answers — a pulse of warmth that travels up Thistle's arm into her chest and is felt, faintly, by Cal where his cut palm is still resting against her sternum.
Closing image. The three of them at the Mother's base — Cal, Thistle, the sapling between them. Bract pressed against Thistle's hip. The mist gone. The light slanted to mid-afternoon and the color of honey. Cal's mouth still gold at the corner where he kissed Thistle through the sap. Thistle's hand on Cal's cut palm, holding it, the slow gold under the skin of his wrist running in a thin line up his forearm where she traced his vein with one finger on Day 1 in the cabin and changed both their lives without knowing it. They have not spoken much. They do not need to. The forest is quiet around them in the way forests are quiet when they have decided, finally, that the people in them are allowed to go home.


Chapter 9 — What the Forest Found (~1,400 words, running long on purpose)
Setting: Three days after the rite. Cal's cabin in the morning. The trail down to Hollow Creek by midday. The Miller Company office and post office in Hollow Creek. The trail back up at dusk. The cabin again, after dark.
POV: Cal through the morning and the Hollow Creek section. Thistle for the reunion and the love scene. POV-fluid in the aftermath. Closes in her head.
Beats:

The third morning. Thistle has been sleeping in his bed for three days, the cedar sparrow in her hand. He has not left the cabin. He has slept in the chair beside her, or not slept. He has watched her breathe like it is a thing he is helping her do. His left hand has been healing — the cut is closed, the gold seam visible from the heel of his thumb to the inside of his wrist, running along the vein she traced with one finger on Day 1. He has been pressing his right thumb along the gold line, learning the new shape of his own arm. On the third morning she opens her eyes fully and stays open. She is hers again.
The conversation she promised him. She tells him everything. Her full name. Her two hundred years. The Mother. The watching for nineteen days. The glove she lifted from a stump six days before he ever saw her face. The trillium in the cutting clearing — that was me, Caleb, that flower at your knees, that was me. He listens without interrupting. He says, "I knew. By the end of day three I knew, and by the morning you ran I had seen my own bare chest in a mirror and known." He takes off his shirt — first time in three days — and shows her the bruise-shape on his sternum, the matched mark visible from across the bed. Her slim hand goes to it. She lays her palm on the print of her own palm and the print warms under her hand. The matched mark on her own chest warms in answer. Both of them feel the small pulse travel between them. She does not say anything for a long moment. Then she says, formal, low: "I marked you twice, Caleb. Once at the chest, once at the wrist. I did not know I was doing either." He says, "I marked you the night you healed me, didn't I." She says, "Yes." He turns his cut palm up so the gold seam catches the morning light. "And here." "Yes." He puts his marked palm over the print on her chest and her marked chest under his marked hand makes both of them stop breathing.
He has to go down the mountain. He explains, fast and flat, what he has to do. The contract. Hiram. The witnesses. He may be arrested. He may be turned away. He cannot live in this cabin with her without going down once to look the world in the face. She does not argue. She knows he is right. She walks him to the door. She is wearing one of his linen shirts that comes to her knees and nothing under it, her small bare feet on his porch boards. She presses her palm flat to his chest — over the mark, the third beat of the four-beat through-line, two more counts before she earns the fourth — and says, "Come back." He says, "I will," and his name in her formal cadence — I will, Thistle-in-the-Hollow — is what he gives her instead of a kiss at the threshold. He kisses her at the door three times anyway. He goes.
Hollow Creek, the small mercies. Cal walks into the Miller Company office at noon. The contract was burned three days ago in the moss; he has nothing to surrender. He turns himself in for the breach. He offers to work off the sixty dollars. Albert Gould — sixty-one, white-haired, salt-and-pepper moustache, the strong forearms still under his rolled sleeves from his young manhood in the forge — looks at Cal for a long second and says, "Thorne. Sit down. There's mail for you." The clerk does not arrest him. The clerk hands him a letter two weeks old. Samuel's handwriting. Cal opens it standing. The territorial essay prize. Two hundred dollars and an apprenticeship in Portland under a Mr. Halloran, room and board, spring start. "I can send money home now, Cal. You can stop. Please stop." Cal sits down on the bench by Albert's desk and cries in a way he has not cried in twenty-two years. He cries quietly, into his hands, with his shoulders shaking once and not again. Albert pretends to be busy with a ledger. Mae Pritchard comes through from the post office with a handkerchief and hands it to him without looking at his face — red hair escaping from its pins at her temple, slim and soft-hipped and the comfortable pretty she has been all her life — and she goes back to the post office without saying anything else. Albert says, after Cal has gathered himself, "Hiram's in the cell behind the magistrate's. He's been raving about witches for three days. Nobody believes him. Don't go back to the camp, Thorne. There's nothing there for you. Go home." Cal stands up. He says, "Yes sir. Thank you, sir." Albert says, "Go home, son."
The walk back. Cal goes up the mountain in the long slant of late afternoon. The forest is quiet around him in the way it has been quiet around him for three days. He does not think. He just walks. Samuel's letter is folded in his breast pocket where the iridescent scale used to live. He has the gold seam in his wrist and the bruise-shape on his sternum and a brother who has paid his own way out and a woman waiting in his cabin in one of his shirts and nothing else under it. He cannot believe the world has let him keep all of these things at once. He does not yet believe it. He walks.
The porch. She has been on the porch since noon. She has not moved. The three trilliums that closed when she left him three days ago are still closed at her bare feet. The alder leaf is still on the step. Bract has been beside her since dawn. The sky is going the color of a bruise — purple at the horizon, gold higher up — and the first star is in the east. She sees him at the bottom of the trail. He sees her at the same moment. He drops his bag where he stands.
The reunion. She comes off the porch at fae speed without bothering to look human about it. Her dress flares behind her — his linen shirt over her own dress, his hat on her head, small bare feet on the path. He catches her at full force; she nearly knocks him backwards. Her arms around his neck. Her legs around his waist. His hands under her thighs. Her mouth on his mouth and not asking. He turns and walks them up the path to the porch with her wrapped around him and her hat falling off and rolling into the moss and neither of them noticing. As his boots hit the porch step, all three of the closed trilliums open under his feet at once, slow, white, full — the forest finishing the sentence the body of the romance started. He does not see them. He kicks the door open with his heel. He kicks it closed behind them. He does not put her down.
[POV slides to Thistle.] The bed. He carries her three steps to the bed and they go down together. She is already pulling at his shirt — buttons popping, two of them rolling across the floor, Bract investigating one of them and deciding it is not food. She gets the shirt off his shoulders and pushes it down his arms; he shrugs it off the rest of the way one-handed because his other hand is in her hair. His suspenders next. His belt. She has watched him do all of this in reverse on Day 6 in the dooryard light and she is undoing him faster than he has ever been undone. She is not careful. She does not have to be. He is not careful either. He drags the linen shirt — his shirt — up over her head in one motion and tosses it. Her own dress under it, the laces at the front — he does not unfasten the laces. He puts his teeth on the laces and tears once and the front comes open and her small bare breasts and the print on her sternum are in his face. He puts his mouth on the print and groans into her skin and she makes a sound she did not know her two hundred years had been keeping back for him.
The body that is fully hers tonight. Her glamour is gone. She has not been bothering to hold it since noon. Her wings unfurl as he gets the rest of her dress off, half-spread against the quilt — iridescent in the lamplight, the colors moving when she does. Her freckles in bark-patterns down her sternum and her ribs and the insides of her slender thighs. Her hair to her hips. Her eyes the green of sun through new leaves with the gold of his sap running through them in faint threads now. He has not seen the gold in her eyes before. He stops. He stares. She says, ragged, low, "Caleb. Do not stop. Do not ever stop. I have been waiting three days." He does not stop.
The first time tonight. He is over her, his weight on his forearms, her small hands at his back. Three days of holding her unconscious and not touching her like this; both of them are starving. He enters her almost without asking — they are past asking — and the sound she makes goes through him like the sound the Mother made when the axe hit her, except that this one is her body welcoming, her body claiming, her body saying yes, yes, yes in a register he can feel in his chest under the mark. She is wrapped around him completely — legs at his waist, one hand fisted in his hair, the other splayed flat over his cut palm where it has braced on the bed beside her shoulder, gold to print. The matched marks on both their chests, pressed together, go warm. Then warmer. Then hot. Then visibly glowing — gold light through the thin skin of his sternum, gold light through hers, the bedroom lit dim gold from inside their bodies. He says, into her ear, "Look at us. Thistle, look at us. We are lit." She comes apart fast — her wings beating once, hard, against the mattress — and he follows three thrusts later with his face pressed to her throat and her name in his teeth. They are both shaking. He stays in her, on her, his forehead at her temple, for a long count. She kisses his ear. She says, low, "Again."
The second time, his mouth. He pulls out slow. Slides down the bed. He kisses the print on her sternum on the way down — one finger of it, the palm, the heel. He kisses her ribs. Her stomach. The bark-pattern freckles at her hipbones. The insides of her slender thighs. He takes a long time. He learned how to do this on her in Ch. 5 and he has been thinking about it for ten days and now he has her bare in lamplight and he is going to do it properly. Female-gaze choreography: his shoulders between her thighs, his hand splayed wide and flat across her belly to keep her there, his other hand laced with hers at her hip, his mouth patient and attentive and almost obscenely focused. She fists her free hand in his hair. She does not get to be quiet about it. Her wings beat slow against the mattress, then unfold further, then come down around him like a canopy — iridescent, dim-gold-lit, full-spread. He has her under a roof of her own body. She comes a second time, longer than the first, his name in her formal cadence cracking in her throat — Caleb Daniel Thorne — and he laughs once into her, wrecked and proud, and does not stop until she is shaking enough to pull at his hair to ask him to.
The third time, her on top. He climbs back up the bed. She rolls him onto his back. She sits up — bare, fae, wings spread wide above her like a stained-glass window in lamplight, hair down her back — and she lowers herself onto him slow. She is the one setting the pace now. He is staring at her like a man at a saint. His hands on her hips. His thumbs at the bark-pattern freckles at her hipbones. She rides him slow at first — eye to eye, the wings rising and falling with her breath, her slim hand spread flat over the print on his chest the whole time, holding the mark against him the way she has held her own hand against her own sternum for chapters. He says, "Thistle, look at me," and she does. He says, "You are the most beautiful thing the world has made," and she does not laugh at him, because she has been alive two hundred years and she knows that he means it the way men mean things they will mean for the rest of their lives. She breaks him on his back in his own bed. He comes with her name in his mouth and his thumb pressed hard to her pulse at her wrist — the same wrist, the same pulse, the print of his thumb she has been carrying since the night of the woods, the body's full circle. She follows him within seconds, head thrown back, wings full open above them, gold light from her chest and her eyes and the seam in his wrist filling the cabin enough that the lamps are unnecessary. She collapses forward onto him. Her wings come down around both of them like a folded tent. The marks on both their chests are hot against each other.
Aftermath. Long quiet. Her hair across his chest. His arm under her shoulders. His thumb tracing the print on her sternum — not fading, never going to fade. Her finger on the gold seam at his wrist. They do not speak for a long time. Outside the cabin window, in the dark, a cluster of trillium opens at the porch step where the three had reopened at his boot — there are seven flowers now where there were three this morning. Neither of them sees. Bract, from the floor, sees and yawns.
The conversation. Eventually she lifts her head. She traces the corner of his mouth with her thumb and finds the faint gold there, three days old, where he kissed her through the Mother's sap. "You drank," she says. "I drank." She kisses the gold off. She says, "Caleb. You're mine. I'm yours. That's it." He says, "Then that's it," and she does not know — neither of them knows — that those four words are the same four claims he made into the Mother's wound three days ago: I'm hers. I'm yours. Take all of it. The romance and the rite are still the same words and they are still being said by accident.
He tells her about Samuel. He reaches over the side of the bed to his coat and pulls out the letter. She reads it pressed against his chest, her wings folded around them both, his hand stroking her hair. When she gets to the line "You can stop, please stop," she puts her face into his throat and stays there for a long count. She does not know what to do with the size of what the forest has given them back. He says, "It worked, Thistle. It worked. I asked the forest to take all of it and find another way and the forest found another way." She says, into his throat, "Of course it did, Caleb. The forest loves you." He laughs, hoarse. He says, "Does it." She says, "It does. I do." She does not say the forest loves him because she does; she does not have to. He understands.
Closing image, Thistle's POV. The lamp has burned low. The cabin is dim gold from the marks on their chests and the seam in his wrist, fading slowly back to skin color as the bodies cool. Bract is asleep at the foot of the bed. Cal's breathing has gone slow and even against her temple. She is looking up at the ceiling — boards he hewed himself, boards he counted across from her three weeks ago on his floor when he was making himself not look at her in his bed. She thinks, calm: I have been alive for two hundred years. I have not been alive until tonight. She thinks: I will not let him grow old without me. Whatever I have to give the Mother, I will give it. She thinks: Tomorrow I will tell him that. She kisses the corner of his mouth where the last of the gold is. He does not wake. She closes her eyes.


Chapter 10 — The Sapling (~500–700 words)
Setting: One year later. Late autumn 1898. The cabin, the porch, the chopping stump behind it. Dusk into early dark.
POV: Cal, with the final beat in Thistle.
Beats:

The cabin has a second room. Cal built it himself, badly — the door catches on the floor and the south wall is a half-inch out of plumb — and Thistle loves it because he built it. The porch is overgrown with white trilliums. They bloom year-round. The few visitors who have come up the mountain have remarked on the strangeness; Cal tells them the porch is a microclimate. Thistle smiles into her tea.
The warden's year. Cal walks the Greenwood five days of seven for the Cascade Conservation Society — forty dollars a month to keep the Miller men out and the small thefts down. Samuel sends another forty each spring from his teacher's apprenticeship in Portland. They are not rich. They are also not hungry, which Cal still cannot get over. He keeps the letters from Samuel in a tin under the bed. Three so far this year. He has read them all more times than he will admit.
Samuel. Has come up the mountain three times since spring. He has met Thistle — twenty-three, five-foot-eleven, the schoolteacher version of Cal with the wire-rim glasses settled into the bridge of his nose and the ink stain at the side of his right middle finger that he never quite scrubs off. He took it well, in the schoolteacher's way — he asked careful questions for an afternoon, drank two cups of tea, and at the end of the visit told Cal, "She is good for you. I am not equipped to ask further than that." He plays the tin whistle Thistle left on the Mother's roots two autumns ago and that she returned to him on his first visit. He did not ask how she knew it was the one his mother gave him at three years old. He plays in the evenings on the porch. The forest goes quiet to listen. Bract puts his chin on Samuel's knee for it.
Bract. Grey on the muzzle. Sleeps most of the day. Still does not let Thistle leave the cabin alone. Cal teases her about it. She pretends to be annoyed. She is not annoyed.
Mae Pritchard and Eli Brennan. Mae married Eli — twenty-nine, six-foot, blond going to brown, easy-tempered, beard like wheat, soft-handed in the way a blacksmith can be when he has loved his work since he was apprenticed at fourteen — in February. Mae had a girl in October: Iris, after her mother. She sent a custard pie up the mountain when she came home from her churching. Cal ate it without irony and wrote a thank-you note in his careful schoolboy hand. Mae sent another pie a month later, unprompted. Cal cried a little, in private, with the pie tin in his lap.
Morrow. Every full moon. She lands on the porch railing in crow form and shifts to half-form to sit — tall and severe and dark, the feathered cape across her shoulders still moving in the wind that is not moving the porch. She calls Cal logger now without spite. He has brought her tea four times. She has accepted it four times. She has not drunk it any of them. She has also not thrown it. Last full moon she sat with Thistle on the porch step among the trilliums for an hour and told her a story about her sister — Thistle's mother, two hundred and forty years gone — that Thistle had never heard. Morrow looked at Thistle's face for a long count afterward and saw the small fixed thing in her niece's freckles that has not shifted in nine months. She did not name what she saw. She lifted into the air as a crow and flew east.
The stone. Still at the Mother's base. The iridescent scale still catches seven colors. The dried trillium has not decayed. Thistle visits the stone at dawn. She puts her palm to the Mother's bark beside the sapling — waist-high now, growing a foot a month, leaning toward the cabin in a way the rest of the forest is too polite to comment on — and she reports the day's tending in her formal cadence. The Mother answers in pulses. Thistle does not let herself think about how long the Mother will live with one less keeper. She refuses the thought every morning.
The present moment. Late afternoon. Cal is splitting wood behind the cabin. Shirt off. Suspenders at his hips. Sweat at his temples and the small of his back and the line of his collarbone. The bruise-mark on his sternum has faded to a pale gold-tinted shadow — visible only in a certain slant of light. The gold seam in his left wrist has gone nearly silver, visible in the same slant. He has been splitting for an hour. He does not need this much wood. He is doing it because she has been watching him from the porch for forty minutes and he likes to be watched.
The walk-over. She knows for three weeks now. She has not found the words. She does not know what fae cadence covers this — she has been alive two centuries and held many fae children at their naming, and none of them have been hers, and the lineage she has memorized for two hundred years does not have a phrase for I am carrying a child for a man who is going to live sixty years. So she does not use a phrase. She comes off the porch with his hat on her head. She crosses to him on small bark-freckled feet. He stops when he sees her come; he sets the axe against the stump. She takes the hat off her head and puts it on his — Keep it, he had said in his cabin a year and a month ago, and she did not, and now she is giving it back, which is also a yes. He laughs once, surprised. She does not laugh. She puts her slim palm flat on his bare chest, on the faded print, the way she has done in this exact spot more times than either of them has counted in a year. The print warms under her hand. She feels the matched print on her own chest warm in answer. He sees her face. He looks down at her hand. He looks at her stomach. He looks back at her face.
The reveal. He does not need her to say it. He sits down hard on the stump. He covers his face with his hands for a long count. When he takes his hands away he is crying and laughing both at once. He pulls her down onto his lap and presses his mouth to the print on her sternum through her dress and stays there. She puts her hand on the back of his head and holds him there until his shoulders stop shaking.
The names. When he lifts his head, his voice is wrecked. He says, "Marin Whitlow Thorne. If she's a girl. After my mother." Thistle says, "Yes." He says, "Daniel Samuel Thorne. If he's a boy. After both of them." Thistle says, "Yes." She does not add a fae name. He notices the absence and does not ask about it. He kisses the inside of her wrist where his thumb has rested through a year of mornings. The gold-silver seam at his wrist warms against her pulse.
The second sparrow. Inside the cabin, on the table where Cal has been whittling in the evenings of this past month — without telling her why, though she knew — a second cedar sparrow lies finished, smaller than the first. The matched pair. He will give it to her tonight. He will say something half-coherent. She will close her small hand around it the same way she closed her hand around its sister a year ago.
Closing image, Thistle's POV. Full dark. The porch trilliums open and white in the cold. Bract asleep through the doorway on the rag rug. Cal sitting on the chopping stump with her in his lap, his hat back on his head, his forehead pressed to her sternum. The faded gold light in his wrist warm against her hip. She watches his hands work the air at her waist — the hands she has been watching for two autumns and a winter and a spring and a summer — and she thinks:

I have been alive for two hundred years and I did not know I was lonely until he said sorry to a tree. I did not know I was a body until he put his mouth on my throat. I did not know I was anyone's until I was his. I will be his for as many years as I have left, and I have chosen those years, and the choosing was the easiest thing I have done in two centuries.
She puts her hand over his hand at her hip. He turns his face up to her. "Thistle-in-the-Hollow," he says, very quiet, into the underside of her jaw. She says into his hair, "Caleb Daniel Thorne." The matched marks on both their chests go warm one last time. The sapling at the Mother's scar, a half-mile east in the dark, puts out one more leaf.

Sensual Through-Lines (Prose-Pass Tags)
Threads that should be hit in nearly every chapter, not only at the marked beats:

Touch-starvation as escalation. Every physical contact between them is louder than it would be for ordinary people. Track which body parts have been touched in which chapter so the next escalation feels earned (wrist → hand → forearm → shoulder → throat → mouth → everything).
His hands as the recurring object of her gaze. Calluses, veins on the forearm, the way they move on the axe haft, the way they move on her. Established Ch. 1 (the stolen glove she has been holding at night), paid through every subsequent chapter.
His restraint as her undoing. He is the one who stops, asks, steps back, looks away. She is the one who closes the distance. Female-gaze dynamic: his honor is the heat.
Her glamour as a barometer. It cracks when she is affected. Tiny tells — a flicker of wing-shimmer, a freckle resolving wrong, a flower blooming behind her — track her arousal and her tenderness through the early chapters before the full reveal earns its weight.
Her body parity with his. Slender frame, small high breasts, narrow waist, hipbones visible at the bark-pattern freckle line, long lean legs, small bare bark-freckled feet, the curve of her throat, the parted lip. She is the heroine — described as fully as he is, returned to often.
Side-cast female gaze. Samuel, Hiram, Albert Gould, Eli Brennan, even Daniel Thorne in memory — each gets a physical descriptor when they enter a scene. The world is full of bodies; the camera notices them.
Smell as memory. Cedar smoke, sweat, soap, leather, his coat, his blanket, his hair. Romantasy convention; pay it.


Sequel Bone
Subtly threaded through Ch. 10 — not announced, not explained, not closed. The reader who notices will see it; the reader who does not will read peace.

Morrow's silent grief. She sees the small fixed thing in Thistle's freckle-pattern that has not shifted in nine months and does not name it. She has the vocabulary; she keeps it.
The freckles that no longer shift. Thistle's glamour-barometer of the early chapters has gone quiet. Her body has settled into a single fixed pattern. The text presents this as peace; the second reading is a body that has chosen to stop being fae.
Thistle's morning refusal of the thought — how long the Mother will live with one less keeper. One line. She is now a finite resource for the forest, and the forest is going to outlive her by less than it should have.
The absent fae name for the child. Cal notices, does not ask. The child will be human-tracked, not fae-tracked.
The closing thought. "As many years as I have left." True of any couple in love. True of her in a different way.

The sequel, if written, would be the daughter's book (The Greenwood's Keeper), set thirty years later, with Thistle aging on a human timeline and a half-fae child raised at the Mother's roots.
